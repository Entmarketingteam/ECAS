## Executive Summary

The global Aviation MRO market exceeds $100 billion annually and is growing at 4-5% CAGR, driven by an aging global fleet, accelerating post-pandemic air travel recovery, and increasing regulatory complexity. Within the United States, over 4,700 FAA-certificated repair stations operate across all segments — from commercial airline heavy maintenance facilities to niche component overhaul shops. Despite operating in a sophisticated, safety-critical industry, the vast majority of MRO companies have virtually no outbound sales function. They survive on airline contracts, OEM referral networks, and word-of-mouth — a model increasingly at risk as fleet operators diversify MRO sourcing and new entrants enter the market. This creates a compelling, under-served opportunity for a cold email lead generation agency: connecting MRO shops with fleet operators, charter companies, corporate flight departments, and cargo operators who need new or backup maintenance providers. Decision-makers are reachable on LinkedIn and via Apollo, deals are substantial (retainers of $45K-$80K/year), and the typical MRO operator has never been approached with a credible, ROI-framed lead generation offer.

## Part 1: Industry Economics and Business Model Analysis

### Revenue Model

Aviation MRO companies generate revenue through a mix of time-and-materials (T&M) billing and fixed-price maintenance agreements. Commercial shops serving airlines may also operate under power-by-the-hour (PBH) contracts, where payment is tied to engine or aircraft flight hours. Component overhaul shops typically charge flat overhaul rates per unit, while heavy maintenance facilities bill based on man-hours at loaded labor rates ($85-$250/hour depending on facility tier and work scope).

| Metric | Small Shop (GA/Biz Jet) | Mid-Market MRO | Large Commercial MRO |
|--------|------------------------|----------------|----------------------|
| Annual Revenue | $3M - $15M | $15M - $100M | $100M - $1B+ |
| Gross Margin | 28-38% | 30-40% | 25-35% |
| Operating Margin | 8-15% | 10-18% | 8-14% |
| Net Profit | 5-10% | 7-14% | 5-10% |
| Typical Project Value | $50K - $500K | $500K - $5M | $5M - $50M+ |
| Customer LTV (airline) | $2M - $20M/yr | $5M - $50M/yr | $50M - $500M/yr |

**Cash flow patterns:** MRO companies often require significant working capital to finance parts inventory and WIP before billing. Airlines pay on 30-60 day terms; bizjet operators often pay faster. Seasonal peaks occur in Q1 and Q3 as airlines schedule heavy checks during low-travel periods.

**Financial indicators of a good client:**
- Revenue above $5M/year (can afford a $3,750-$6,500/month retainer)
- Existing but stagnant customer roster (5-15 airline/operator clients — they want more but aren't hunting)
- Open shop capacity (MRO shops with utilization below 80% are actively motivated)
- Recently added capabilities (new ratings create need to win work to amortize investment)

**Discovery questions for financial qualification:**
1. "What percentage of your capacity are you running at right now — are you turning away work?"
2. "Do you have airline customers, or are you primarily working with corporate and charter operators?"
3. "When a new airline or fleet operator approaches you, what's your typical conversion to an actual contract?"
4. "Have you had situations where you invested in a new capability or additional staff and then struggled to fill the capacity?"

### Minimum Revenue Threshold
A shop generating $5M+ in annual revenue can comfortably allocate $45K-$80K/year on lead generation retainer — representing less than 1% of top-line revenue with realistic expectation of 5-20x ROI if even one new airline maintenance agreement results.

## Part 2: Customer Journey and Buying Behavior

### MRO's Internal Customer: Fleet Operators

MRO shops sell to: commercial airlines, regional carriers, cargo operators (FedEx/UPS networks and regional subcontractors), charter/air taxi operators, corporate flight departments, fractional ownership programs (NetJets, Flexjet), government and military operators, and leasing companies doing end-of-lease checks.

**Their customers' typical buying journey:**
- **Stage 1 — Awareness (Trigger):** Fleet expansion, existing MRO capacity constraints, regulatory finding at current MRO, cost reduction initiative, new aircraft type requiring different ratings
- **Stage 2 — Consideration:** Reference checks within industry networks (aviation is small), capability/rating verification via FAA registry, site visit and audit
- **Stage 3 — Decision:** Negotiation of master services agreement (MSA), rate card approval, insurance and quality documentation review

**Typical sales cycle for MRO-to-operator deals:** 60-180 days for an airline; 14-45 days for corporate/charter.

**Primary purchase triggers for fleet operators:**
1. Existing MRO at capacity or delivery delays
2. Geographic expansion requiring maintenance closer to new hub
3. Cost reduction mandate from airline procurement
4. New aircraft type added to fleet requiring different ratings
5. Regulatory audit finding deficiencies at current MRO provider
6. Lease return check approaching with no provider confirmed

**Key stakeholders:**
- **Director of Maintenance / VP Maintenance:** Technical decision-maker, cares about quality, turnaround time, and regulatory standing
- **VP Operations / COO:** Approves vendor relationships, cares about schedule reliability and cost
- **Procurement/Sourcing:** Runs vendor qualification, RFP process, rate card benchmarking
- **CFO:** Final authority on large contracts; interested in total cost per flight hour

**Internal approval process for hiring a lead gen agency:**
MRO owners are entrepreneurial — many are A&P mechanics who built their own shops. They make decisions autonomously for amounts under $100K/year. Board approval is rare below $250K. The real hurdle is skepticism that "marketing" can work in aviation, not budget authorization.

## Part 3: Discovery Call Architecture and Conversation Framework

### 5-Section Framework

**Section 1 — Rapport (5-10 min)**
Open by referencing a specific industry moment: "I noticed you just earned your EASA Part-145 approval — that's a significant investment. What opened that door for you?"

Alternative openers:
- "I saw you were at MRO Americas last month — what were the big themes you heard from the operators?"
- "Your shop has been in [City] for [X] years — were you there when [regional carrier] was still routing heavy checks through that area?"

**Word-for-word opening script:**
> "Hey [Name], thanks for getting on. I'll be brief and direct. I work with a handful of MRO shops — shops like yours — that do excellent work but rely almost entirely on word of mouth and existing airline relationships to grow. My question for you today is simple: if I could show you a repeatable system for getting in front of Director of Maintenance contacts at charter operators, cargo carriers, and regional airlines who don't have a primary MRO relationship locked up — would that be worth 20 minutes?"

**Section 2 — Diagnosis (15-20 min)**
Diagnostic questions with good vs. concerning answer analysis:

| Question | Good Answer | Concerning Answer |
|----------|-------------|-------------------|
| "Walk me through how you win a new airline customer today." | "Honestly, mostly referrals — an airline hears about us from another carrier and calls." | "We have a sales team that cold calls airlines regularly." |
| "What percentage of your capacity is booked 90 days out?" | "About 70% — we have room but we're not sure how to fill it." | "We're 100% booked for 18 months." |
| "Have you tried any outbound sales or marketing before?" | "Not really — aviation isn't like that, people call us." | "Yes, we hired a marketing firm and wasted $50K." |
| "What type of customers are you trying to add?" | "Charter operators and cargo carriers — we've been mainly domestic airlines." | "We only want to work with top-tier carriers." |
| "What does a new airline contract mean to your bottom line?" | "$500K to $2M in annual revenue — it's transformative." | "It depends — some are barely worth it." |

**Section 3 — Future State (10-15 min)**
> "Let me paint the picture of what we build for you. We identify 200-500 Director of Maintenance contacts at operators who match your capability profile — right aircraft types, right geography. We write cold email sequences that speak their language: turnaround guarantees, regulatory credibility, specific capabilities. Our goal is 8-15 qualified conversations per month. You close what you close. What would 2-3 new operator relationships per quarter do for your capacity utilization?"

**Section 4 — Budget & Authority (5 min)**
> "The investment for what we're describing is in the $45,000-$75,000 range annually, structured as a monthly retainer. That typically represents less than one maintenance visit's worth of revenue from a new operator. Are you the one who makes this call, or does your CFO or a partner need to be in the room before we finalize?"

**Section 5 — Next Steps (5 min)**
> "Here's what I'd suggest: let me send over a sample targeting list — 50 operators that match your profile — and a mock email sequence so you can see exactly what your prospects would receive. If it looks right, we do a 90-day pilot. Sound fair?"

## Part 4: Marketing Landscape and Current Practices

### How MRO Companies Currently Acquire Customers

| Channel | Effectiveness | Description |
|---------|--------------|-------------|
| Industry referrals | Very High | Dominant channel. Airline maintenance directors have tight networks. |
| Trade shows (MRO Americas, MRO Europe) | High | Key meeting point, but passive — shops wait for operators to approach them. |
| OEM partnerships | Medium-High | Engine/airframe OEMs refer operators to authorized service centers. |
| FAA registry visibility | Medium | Operators search FAA ARSA database for certificated shops. |
| Word-of-mouth | Medium | Strong within segments, but slow and geographically limited. |
| Digital/SEO/Advertising | Very Low | Almost non-existent — aviation buyers don't Google for MRO providers. |
| Cold email/outbound | Near zero | Essentially no MRO shop runs a disciplined outbound sales motion. |

**Marketing sophistication assessment:** Low. Most MRO companies have outdated websites, no CRM, no dedicated sales staff, and no marketing budget as a percentage of revenue. The industry runs on relationships and reputation — which means the first agency to systematize outbound will have an enormous first-mover advantage.

**Marketing budget as % of revenue:** 0.1-0.5% for most shops. Essentially zero in dedicated outbound/digital.

**Dominant agency failure patterns:** Generalist digital agencies have tried to serve MRO with SEO and Google Ads — failing because their customers don't search online. The failure pattern is: agency delivers website traffic, MRO owner sees no qualified leads, cancels after 3 months.

> **The single most common agency failure in aviation MRO:** "They built us a beautiful website and ran ads, and we got zero calls from operators. Airlines don't Google for MRO shops."

| Regulatory Body | Acronym | Relevance to Marketing |
|-----------------|---------|----------------------|
| Federal Aviation Administration | FAA | Issues Repair Station Certificates (Part 145) — core credibility asset |
| European Union Aviation Safety Agency | EASA | Part 145 approval opens European airline market — major differentiator |

**Marketing-specific compliance:**
- MRO shops cannot falsely claim capabilities not covered by their FAA certificate
- Claims about turnaround time must be defensible — overpromising TAT is a regulatory and legal liability

**Script for addressing compliance in sales conversations:**
> "Everything we put in front of operators will be reviewed by you before it goes out. We're not making capability claims — we're simply getting your name in front of people who need what you already do. Think of it as us finding you the meeting; your reputation and your certificate close the deal."

## Part 7: Industry Vocabulary and Cultural Fluency

| Term/Acronym | Meaning | Context & Usage |
|--------------|---------|----------------|
| MRO | Maintenance, Repair & Overhaul | The industry umbrella term — use freely |
| C-Check | Heavy maintenance cycle (~6,000-8,000 flight hours) | Mid-level heavy check — mention when targeting narrowbody operators |
| D-Check | Full aircraft overhaul (~25,000-30,000 hours) | The heaviest check — positions shop as capable of full overhaul |
| TAT | Turnaround Time | Critical metric for operators — shorter TAT = aircraft back in service faster |
| AOG | Aircraft on Ground | Emergency priority status — means revenue loss for operator every hour |
| Part 145 | FAA Repair Station Certificate | Required to work on certificated aircraft — always reference this |
| A&P | Airframe & Powerplant mechanic | Core labor certification — used when discussing workforce |
| IA | Inspection Authorization | Additional FAA cert allowing mechanics to approve major repairs |
| PMA | Parts Manufacturer Approval | Aftermarket part approval — alternative to OEM parts |
| STC | Supplemental Type Certificate | Allows modifications not in original aircraft design |
| AD | Airworthiness Directive | FAA-mandated maintenance requirements — compliance is non-negotiable |
| MSG-3 | Maintenance Steering Group 3 | Methodology underlying scheduled maintenance programs |
| CAMO | Continuing Airworthiness Management Organization | European equivalent of US maintenance program management |
| AMM | Aircraft Maintenance Manual | OEM-published document governing all maintenance procedures |
| SDR | Service Difficulty Report | FAA reporting system for maintenance anomalies |

**Cultural landmines to avoid:**

1. **Never say "fix planes"** — aviation professionals say "maintain," "overhaul," or "service." "Fix" implies something was broken negligently.
2. **Don't call it "marketing" in the pitch** — call it "business development" or "customer acquisition." Aviation MRO culture views marketing as something consumer brands do, not serious technical companies.
3. **Don't minimize regulatory complexity** — saying "how hard can it be to find new customers" signals you don't understand what it takes to qualify as an approved MRO provider.
4. **Never confuse FAA Part 135 (operations) with Part 145 (maintenance)** — conflating these reveals fundamental ignorance of the regulatory structure.
5. **Don't call customers "clients"** — in aviation MRO, they are "customers," "operators," or "carriers." "Client" sounds like you're confused with a law firm.

## Part 10: Pain Points and Deeper Business Challenges

1. **"We're great at maintenance but terrible at sales."**
Most MRO operators are ex-mechanics or ex-military aviation technicians who built their business on technical excellence. They have no sales infrastructure, no CRM, and no idea how to systematically develop new customer relationships. Connection: we give them a sales motion without requiring them to hire or become salespeople.

2. **"We have too many eggs in one basket — one airline is 60% of our revenue."**
Customer concentration is the existential risk for mid-market MRO shops. When an anchor airline customer gets acquired, consolidates, or cancels a contract, shops can go from thriving to fighting for survival in months. Connection: pipeline diversification is a survival strategy, not just a growth strategy.

3. **"We invested in new capabilities and ratings but haven't filled the capacity."**
Earning an EASA approval, adding a new engine rating, or completing a new hangar costs $500K-$5M. The ROI depends on winning customers for those capabilities. Connection: we're the commercialization engine for their capability investment.

4. **"We lose people because we can't promise stable work."**
A&P mechanics are in short supply — competition is fierce from airlines, OEMs, and other shops. Workforce stability requires predictable work, which requires reliable customer pipeline. Connection: diversified customer base = stable employment = retention.

5. **"We can't compete with the big MROs on price."**
Large MRO networks (ST Engineering, AAR, Lufthansa Technik) undercut independents on cost through scale. Independent shops win on specialization, responsiveness, and relationships — but only with customers who know they exist. Connection: we make sure the right operators know the shop exists before they default to the big guys.
